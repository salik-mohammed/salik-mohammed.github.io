Project 02 / Multilingual quality review

Three errors an English-only reviewer cannot see

Machine output into Hindi, Urdu and Marathi can be fluent, grammatical and still wrong in ways that never surface in the English source. These are the three failures I look for first, each one invisible to a reviewer working from the English alone.

Working languages: English, Hindi, Urdu, Marathi. Examples constructed for this report.

Why the English source hides these

English carries less grammatical information than the three target languages do. It has one word for "you" where Hindi, Urdu and Marathi have three levels of respect. Its past tense does not mark the gender of the speaker. Its "we" does not say whether the listener is included.

So the model has to supply information the source never gave it. When it guesses wrong the output stays fluent, which is the problem: there is nothing to flag unless someone reads the target language and knows who is speaking to whom.

Error 1. Speaker gender defaults to masculine

Hindi

English source

I went to the market.

Context given to the translator: the speaker is a woman.

Model output

मैं बाज़ार गया।

main bazaar gaya.

Grammatical. The verb is masculine, so the sentence says a man went.

Correct

मैं बाज़ार गई।

main bazaar gayi.

One letter changes. The verb now agrees with a female speaker.

Hindi, Urdu and Marathi verbs agree with the gender of the subject. English past tense does not, so the model has nothing to work from and falls back on masculine. The output is correct Hindi. It is simply about someone else.

Where it costs: any first-person content where the speaker is known to be female. Product reviews, chat assistants, voice agents, subtitled dialogue, personal testimonials. Every sentence a woman says in the past tense is a place this can go wrong.

Error 2. Respect level drops to the familiar form

Hindi and Urdu

English source

Please confirm your booking.

Context: a message from a company to a customer.

Model output, Hindi

अपनी बुकिंग कन्फर्म करो।

apni booking confirm karo.

The "tum" level. This is how you speak to a child or a close friend. To a customer it reads as an order from someone who does not respect them.

Correct, Hindi

कृपया अपनी बुकिंग की पुष्टि करें।

krpaya apni booking ki pushti karein.

The "aap" level, with the polite imperative. Correct for a company addressing a customer.

Model output, Urdu

اپنی بکنگ کنفرم کرو۔

apni booking confirm karo.

Same failure, same register.

Correct, Urdu

براہ کرم اپنی بکنگ کی تصدیق کریں۔

baraah-e-karam apni booking ki tasdeeq karein.

Formal imperative, correct for customer-facing text.

English "please" does not carry a respect level, so it cannot tell the model which of the three forms to use. Back-translated into English both versions return "please confirm your booking", which means a reviewer checking by back-translation will pass the rude one.

Where it costs: customer support, banking and insurance notifications, government and health messaging, anything addressed to an elder. This error does not read as a translation mistake to the person receiving it. It reads as the company being rude.

Error 3. The ambiguous "we"

Marathi

English source

We should leave now.

Reading 1, listener included

आपण आता निघायला हवं।

aapan aata nighaayla havam.

You and I should go.

Reading 2, listener excluded

आम्ही आता निघायला हवं।

aamhi aata nighaayla havam.

We are going. You are not part of this.

Marathi splits "we" into inclusive and exclusive. English does not, so the model picks one and there is a fifty percent chance it is the wrong one. Both outputs are fluent Marathi, and both back-translate to the same English sentence.

Unlike the first two errors this one has no safe default. The only fix is context, which means flagging it as an ambiguity in the source rather than as a translation error. That distinction matters in annotation work: this is a case where the right output is to ask, not to pick.

Where it costs: dialogue, subtitles, instructions, meeting notes, any content where it matters who is being included. Choosing wrong does not sound wrong. It quietly changes who the sentence is about.

How I check for these

  • Read the target language first, before the source. Reading the English first sets an expectation and the eye then confirms it rather than checking it.
  • Do not rely on back-translation. All three errors here survive a round trip into English intact, which is exactly why they reach production.
  • For every first-person past-tense sentence, ask who is speaking. If the brief does not say, that is a question for the client, not a guess.
  • For every sentence addressed to a reader, ask who is speaking to whom. Register is set by the relationship, not by the words in the source.
  • Separate what is a model error from what is missing context. The Marathi case is the second kind, and reporting it as the first wastes everyone's time.
