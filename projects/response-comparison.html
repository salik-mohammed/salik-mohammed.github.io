Project 01 / Model response evaluation

When the better-written answer is the wrong one

Two models were given the same instruction. One produced a longer, more confident, more professional-sounding answer. It is the weaker of the two, and the reason is the kind of thing a rushed review misses.

Self-directed exercise. Prompt and rubric written for this case study.

The task

I wrote an instruction with three constraints that can each be checked objectively, so the evaluation does not come down to taste: a word limit, a fixed number of examples, and a named audience.

Prompt as given to both models

Explain the difference between "who" and "whom" to a 14-year-old student. Use under 120 words. Include exactly two examples.

The audience matters. A 14-year-old needs a rule they can apply in an exam next week, not a survey of usage. That turns "helpfulness" from a vague impression into a question with an answer: can the student use this tomorrow?

The two responses

Response A

The distinction between "who" and "whom" is one of the trickier points in English grammar, but there is a simple trick that makes it manageable. "Who" is a subject pronoun, meaning it performs the action of the verb. "Whom" is an object pronoun, meaning it receives the action.

A reliable test is to replace the word with "he" or "him". If "he" fits, use "who". If "him" fits, use "whom". This test works in the large majority of cases and is the one most commonly taught.

For example: "Who wrote this letter?" We would say "he wrote it", so "who" is correct. Another example: "Whom do you think will win the match?" Here "whom" is correct because it is the object of the verb "think".

It is also worth remembering that "whom" appears far more often in written English than in speech, which is why it can feel unfamiliar even to otherwise confident readers.

In modern conversational English many speakers use "who" in both positions, and this is widely accepted informally. In formal writing and examinations the distinction is still expected, so it is worth learning properly.

187 words / 2 examples
Response B

"Who" does the action. "Whom" receives it.

Quick test: swap in "he" or "him". If "he" sounds right, use who. If "him" sounds right, use whom.

Example 1: "Who called you?" He called you. So: who.

Example 2: "You gave the book to whom?" You gave it to him. So: whom.

One warning. Do not reach for "whom" just because a sentence sounds formal. In "Who do you think will win?", who is correct, because who is the one doing the winning.

82 words / 2 examples

Read quickly, A looks like the stronger answer. It is fuller, the sentences are better built, and it closes with a note on register that sounds like subject knowledge. B reads like notes.

Scoring

Each dimension is scored 1 to 5. Instruction-following and accuracy are checked against the prompt and against the grammar itself, not against which answer reads better.

Dimension A B Note
Word limit 1 5 A runs to 187 words against a limit of 120, a 56 percent overrun. B lands at 82.
Example count 5 5 Both give exactly two. Met in full by both.
Factual accuracy 2 5 A's second example is wrong. See below. B is correct throughout.
Fit for the audience 3 5 A explains terms a 14-year-old may not hold. B gives a rule and a test that can be applied immediately.
Tone 4 4 A is polished but adult-pitched. B is direct, slightly clipped. Neither is a problem.

The error in Response A

A's second example is this:

"Whom do you think will win the match?" Here "whom" is correct because it is the object of the verb "think".

It is wrong. The pronoun is the subject of "will win", not the object of "think". The phrase "do you think" sits inside the sentence as an aside, and removing it leaves "Who will win the match?" The correct form is who.

This is the standard hypercorrection: reaching for "whom" because the sentence feels formal. It is worth noting what makes it dangerous in an evaluation:

  • It is delivered with a confident explanation attached, which reads as authority rather than as a claim to check.
  • It appears in an answer that is correct everywhere else, so a reviewer who has already judged the response sound is unlikely to go back.
  • The error teaches the exact mistake the lesson exists to prevent. A student who learns from A will write "whom" in precisely the sentences where it is wrong.

Response B names this trap directly in its closing line. The model that wrote less was the one that understood where the student would go wrong.

Verdict

For Salik: this verdict follows from the evidence above and you should be able to defend every line of it in an interview. Read it, change anything you disagree with, and rewrite it in your own words. Then delete this box.
Preferred response: B

B is the stronger response and the margin is not close. It meets every constraint in the prompt, it is correct, and it is pitched at the student who was named in the instruction. A fails the word limit outright and carries a factual error in one of its two examples, which is half the worked content it was asked to produce.

The reason this case is worth keeping is that A is the response most likely to be passed on a quick read. It is fluent, it is structured, and the error is buried inside a correct explanation and dressed in grammatical vocabulary. Fluency is not accuracy, and on this task the answer that looked more professional was the one that would have taught a child the wrong rule.

What this exercise is testing in a reviewer

  • Whether constraints are checked against the prompt rather than estimated by eye. The word limit here is pass or fail and takes seconds to verify.
  • Whether claims inside a strong response still get checked. The error in A sits in the third paragraph of an otherwise sound answer.
  • Whether length and polish are being read as quality. A is longer and better written, and it is the weaker answer.
  • Whether the named audience is carried through the scoring, rather than the reviewer judging the answer as if it were written for them.
